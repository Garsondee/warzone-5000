# Theory: geometry (lane GEOMETRY), plain language, growing with the lane

## A loft is a ship's lines plan
A hull is described by a few cross-sections at stations along its length (the body plan of a ship's drawing) and a rule for the surface between them. Maya's *loft* does the same: you give section curves and it skins them. We use straight rulings between sections, so a section is a ring of corner points and the surface is the quad strips between neighbouring rings, each quad split in two triangles. Changing a station's width or height then changes the vehicle smoothly, which is why a design can be "parametric": the parameters are the section numbers.

## Why a mesh must be closed and outward-facing
Three things rely on it. **Volume**: the divergence theorem says the volume of a solid equals a sum over its boundary; for triangles it is the sum of signed tetrahedra against the origin, (a . (b x c)) / 6, the figure the 3D-print toolbox reports. A triangle that faces the wrong way subtracts instead of adds; a hole makes the sum depend on where the origin is. **Armour**: a ray fired through the vehicle crosses an even number of faces if the surface is closed, and the thickness is the sum of in-out chords. **Mass**: centroid and inertia are the first and second moments of the same integral, so geometry, mass and armour agree because they come from one mesh. The inertia tensor is the covariance matrix of the mass distribution (the same matrix a PCA oriented-box fit computes for points), and the parallel-axis theorem is how covariances of parts combine.

## Edge and cavity are curvature and ambient-occlusion bakes, held per vertex
Substance Painter's *curvature* and *ambient occlusion* maps are textures; we have no UVs, so we store the same information at the vertices and let the GPU interpolate between them. That has one consequence the spike measured (`docs/lanes/geometry/spike-g.md`): a value can only change where there is a vertex, so a sharp, narrow edge band needs an extra ring of vertices at the width you want, and a corner's high value runs along the whole edge unless there is a vertex mid-way. Cavity is the fraction of cosine-weighted rays from a point that hit something within 0.5 m. Cosine weighting makes the rays uniform on the unit disc seen from above, so at the foot of a tall wall the blocked share is a circular segment of that disc, which gives a closed-form curve to test against.

## One shell, cut in the section (why the truck stopped looking like a heap of boxes)
The first truck was assembled from boxes, like a kit of cardboard parts: each box is closed and correct, but nothing joins them, so a window can hover off a wall and a mud guard can lie across a wheel like a plank. Real vehicles are built the other way round: a body shell first, then the openings cut into it, then the fittings bolted on.

In a ship's lines plan the hull is described by its sections, and an opening in the hull is just a section whose outline has a notch. That is how the wheel arches work here: the outline of each section is a T (wide lower body, narrower cab on top), and near a wheel the lower outer corner of the outline is notched up to the wheel circle, `y = y_c + sqrt((R + gap)^2 - dz^2)`. Joining the sections gives the arch for free, as a curved vault, with no boolean subtraction (the operation a CAD kernel uses to cut a hole, which needs a robust mesh intersection and is a classic source of numerical trouble). A *sweep* does the same for the lip: take a small rectangular section and rotate it around the wheel axis, which is exactly how you would model a wrapped fender in Maya with a curve and a profile.

Two small algorithms came along. **Ear clipping** triangulates a flat outline that is not convex (the T-shaped nose face): repeatedly cut off a corner triangle that contains no other point, like peeling the corners of a polygon one at a time. **Anchoring** replaces absolute positions with surface offsets: "the mirror arm starts 3 cm inside the cab wall and reaches 17 cm outside it" holds when the truck gets wider, "the mirror is 91 cm from the centre line" does not. A test that every fitting overlaps the hull then turns "looks attached" into something the build can check.

## Making two angles agree: derive one from the other
A window and a windscreen can both look "about right" and still disagree by twenty degrees, and the eye notices. The way to make two lines parallel is not to measure both carefully but to construct the second from the first: the front side window's slanted edge is the windscreen's roof-line ramp, shifted into the cab by the width of the pillar, then cut by the sill and the roof rail. Offsetting a line keeps its direction, so the angles agree to the last bit and stay in agreement when the truck is resized. The same idea sits behind the frame around each pane: a loop swept along the pane's outline with a small profile, mitered at the corners (each corner point moves along the corner's bisector, stretched by 1 / cos of half the turn so that every side of the frame has the same width, the way a picture framer cuts a mitre).

A lean wall (tumblehome) is a shear: every point moves sideways in proportion to its height, which keeps planes planar and parallel lines parallel, so a flat pane sheared by the same amount lies on the leaning wall with no gap. Finally, a rendering lesson from the picture tool: under perspective, 1 / depth (not depth) varies linearly across the screen, which is why texture mapping on early consoles warped (affine mapping) and why a depth test with screen-space depth lets a large wall show through glass a few millimetres in front of it.

## Sockets are connector standards (why one gun can fit many mounts and one mount many hulls)
A modular vehicle is a plug-and-socket problem, the same one USB-C solves: as long as every hull offers the same kind of socket and every mount knows how to plug into it, nobody needs to know what is on the other side. A **socket** is a *frame* (a position plus three perpendicular directions, the way a locator or a joint in Maya has a position and an orientation) with a type and a size. Two directions do all the work: the **normal** (which way the socket faces, out of the part that owns it) and a **reference** (which way is "forward", or "up"), because a position and a normal alone leave the part free to spin like a bolt in a hole. Attaching is then a rigid move: carry the child's mount frame onto the parent's socket frame so that the two normals face each other (a plug faces its socket) and the two references agree. In code that is one composition of transforms, `parent * spin * half_turn * inverse(child_mount)`: take the child to its own mount, flip it to face the socket, then place the whole thing where the socket is. Composing three or four of them gives the chain hull, ring mount, weapon, and every position is the product of the frames along the chain, exactly like a bone hierarchy.

Three details are worth knowing. **Size and kind gate the fit** the way a plug shape does: a 1.0 m turret ring does not go on a 0.9 m socket, and a weapon cannot be bolted to a wheel station; anything legal is allowed and *physics* then judges it (too heavy for the springs, front-heavy, recoil beyond what the hull absorbs). **Left is not right**: a left-hand glove is not a right-hand glove turned round, and a wheel with lugs, a mirror arm or a stowage box on one side must be reflected, not rotated, to serve the other side. Reflecting a mesh reverses its triangle winding (a mirror turns the inside out), so the kernel flips the winding back; an unmirrored copy rotated half a turn would land upside down, which the test for the left socket checks. Last, **only what moves needs a frame of its own**: a fixed collar bolted to the roof is baked into the hull, while the turntable that yaws, the gun that pitches and the barrel that recoils keep their own node frames, so the animation can move them later without cutting the mesh.

## A hull is cut for what it will carry (and how to prove a refactor changed nothing)
A wheel arch is not part of the wheel and not part of the hull's *style*; it is a negotiation. The hull has to be cut for the biggest wheel it will take, and the wheel has to know how far to reach back to the hull's wall. Shared code is the wrong way to settle this (the two families would depend on each other's internals); published numbers are the right way. The hull publishes, at every wheel position, a socket: where the hub is, which way is out, how large a wheel the arch was cut for, and a few named hints (where the inner wall of the wheel well is, how wide a tyre it takes). The wheel generator reads those and nothing else; the knuckle's length is computed from the wall hint, so a different hull with a different wall gives a different knuckle with no change to the wheel code. This is the same idea as a shader reading a material's published parameters: the interface is a list of named numbers.

Changing how something is built without changing what it builds is the riskiest kind of edit, because nothing visibly changes when it goes wrong. The cure is to freeze the output: before the change the generator's outputs are hashed (the golden hash covers every part name, every vertex bit and every triangle index) and the exported files are fingerprinted (the render rig and the glTF file); after the change the same fingerprints must come out. Here they did, bit for bit, even though the left wheels are now mirrored from a right-hand module instead of built mirrored. Two small numeric lessons came with it. A quarter turn computed through a quaternion carries a rounding error of 1e-16, which would leave every wheel with a rotation of "almost nothing"; snapping components that are within a hair of 0, 1/2, 1/sqrt(2) or 1 to those exact numbers keeps frames on the axes exact, so a wheel on an axis-aligned socket is placed with no rotation at all. And a quaternion and its negative are the same rotation (the double cover of the rotation group), so poses are stored with the sign that makes w non-negative, and two ways of arriving at the same placement read identically.

## A mount is a negotiation about size, and a pivot is a frame (the ring mount)
A ring mount on a roof is three things stacked: a **collar** bolted to the hull, a **turntable** that rotates on it, and a **cradle** that holds the gun. In an animation rig this is a chain of joints: the turntable's joint turns about the vertical axis through the centre of the collar's top face (yaw), the gun's joint turns about the trunnion pin (pitch), and the barrel slides along its own axis (recoil). The model therefore stores each moving part in the frame of its own joint, with the joint's position and orientation as a pose: the mesh of the turntable is built around its own origin (the centre of the collar's top face), and the pose says where that origin is on the roof. Parts that never move (the collar) are baked straight into the hull's frame, exactly as you would bake a static prop into the level rather than parent it to a bone.

Sockets carry **sizes**, and the size is what stops nonsense: the roof offers a 0.90 m ring, so a 1.2 m ring mount is refused at attach time with the reason in plain words, and the mount offers a 0.30 m cradle, so a gun whose receiver is 0.42 m wide is refused. Nothing in the roof or the mount knows about any particular gun; the numbers do the work, in the same way a plug that is too big does not fit a socket that is too small. Two details keep a sculpted model honest: a part that passes *through* another (a pin through a receiver, a barrel into its jacket) has no vertex inside the other, so the test that nothing floats samples the midpoints of edges as well as the vertices; and the roof rises a degree along its length, so the collar is sunk 2 cm into it, so that a level ring always meets a tilted roof without a gap on the high side.

## One generator, many guns (presets are just numbers) and what recoil is
A machine gun and an autocannon are the same machine at different sizes: a receiver (the box that holds the bolt and the feed), a barrel, perhaps a jacket around the root of the barrel to carry heat, a muzzle device, a pair of trunnion pins that it swings on, and ammunition boxes. So the generator is written once and a *preset* is only a handful of numbers (receiver size, barrel length and radius, how many boxes), the way a character rig in a game is one skeleton with different proportion sliders. The same trick makes weapons different in kind from one another without new code: a gun with no grips and a sight on top is a remotely fired mount, a gun with spade grips is a manual one, and the data says which.

Recoil is a *prismatic* joint: the barrel group slides back along its own axis and returns, while the receiver and cradle stay put. In the model that is one node with its own frame, whose axis is the bore; all the parts that move with the barrel (barrel, jacket, muzzle device) belong to it, and the rest belong to the pitch node above it. The three joints chain exactly like shoulder, elbow and wrist: turntable yaw, then gun pitch about the trunnion pins, then recoil along the bore. Because each moving part is stored in its own joint's frame, the animation later only has to set three numbers per frame (yaw, pitch, slide) and the whole weapon follows.

## A rig is a tree of frames, and forward kinematics is how you check it
An animation rig stores, for each joint, where it sits relative to its parent (its *rest pose*) and what it does (rotate about an axis, or slide along it). To find where any part is at any moment, walk from the part's node up to the root, multiplying the transforms: world(child) = world(parent) * rest(child) * motion(child, q), which is the same chain of matrices you meet in any skeleton. The export builds that tree from the assembly: each moving part is stored in the frame of its own node, and the node's rest is the node's pose relative to its parent, so the walk reproduces the position the part was assembled at. That is a strong test because it is independent of how the tree was built: put every vertex of every part through the walk at rest and compare with where the assembly put it; any wrong parent, missing offset or reversed order shows up as a part in the wrong place.

The parent of a node is not guessed from geometry (a gun near a turntable is probably on it, but "probably" is how two turrets get their guns swapped); it is recorded when the module is attached. Each part remembers which placement it came from and which node carried the socket it sits on, so a gun on a ring mount's trunnion hangs from that turntable, however many mounts the vehicle has. The sign conventions are the other half of a rig and are tested on the rig itself, not on the code that built it: with the walk set to yaw +0.3 rad the barrel's bore direction must turn to the left, with pitch +0.2 the muzzle must rise, and with recoil +0.1 the barrel must move back along its axis.

## Stretching a region, not the whole (piecewise maps)
If every position on a hull is a fraction of its overall length, then making the truck 1.2 m longer makes the hood, the cab, the doors and the bed all longer together, which is not what a designer means by "a longer bed". The fix is the one a character animator uses for a stretchy limb: cut the length at a few landmarks and give each piece its own scale. Here the cuts are the nose, the cowl, the back wall of the cab and the tail; the template stores a position as a fraction along the whole, and a *map* turns the fraction into metres by finding which piece it lies in, taking its fraction within that piece and multiplying by that piece's length (a piecewise-linear function, like a time-remap curve with three segments). Everything that was anchored by a fraction (a door, the roof hatch, the windscreen) goes through that same map, so it stays where it belongs in its piece however the other pieces are resized; things anchored to an end (bumpers, the tailgate) follow that end.

Two checks keep it honest. With the pieces set to the template's own proportions the map must reproduce the original truck (a map that changes nothing when asked for nothing), and when the bed is lengthened by a stated amount, the pieces in front of it must be bit-for-bit unchanged and what hangs on the tail must move by exactly that amount; the margins beyond the end stations (the allowance for bumpers) keep their own scale, otherwise "1.2 m longer" would quietly become 1.4 m.

## One layout, many skins (why the joints must not change)
A game with several vehicles on screen needs the same machinery to drive all of them, so the *skeleton* (which joint is which, in which order) is fixed and only the *skin* changes: a retargeting step moves the skin's nodes to where the physics says they are by matching joint indices, the way a motion-capture clip is retargeted from one character rig to another with the same bone names. Here the skeleton is four wheel spins, two steering angles and four suspension travels, always front axle left then right, then rear axle left then right. A scout, a utility truck and a heavy truck are three templates for the *shape* that share that skeleton, so the guarantee is structural (the wheels are the same module on the same sockets) and a test checks it by exporting each skin and comparing the joints.

The second lesson is where the numbers come from. A skin that has its own idea of the wheel radius will drift from the physics (the wheel will sink into the ground or float above it in the picture), so the dimensions are read from the same definition the physics uses, and a test fails if the stand-in numbers and the definition ever disagree. A silhouette that reads at a distance is built from proportion, not from detail: the scout is low and open, with no cab, so from the side and above it is a flat runabout; detail (seats, roll bar, spare wheel) only adds interest once you are close.
