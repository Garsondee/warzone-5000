# Theory: soft ground, tracks and skid steering (lane TRACKS)

Plain language; the numbers are checked in `crates/w5k_terramech/tests/`. Not yet sourced against a book: see the UNVALIDATED tags.

## 1. Soil is a spring and a friction wedge
**As a spring (Bekker).** Push a flat plate of width `b` into soft ground and the pressure rises as a power of the depth: `p = (kc/b + kphi) z^n`. The exponent `n` says how the soil stiffens: `n = 1` is a straight spring, `n < 1` a spring that stiffens quickly at first (wet clay), `n > 1` one that softens at first and then firms up (snow). The term `kc/b` falls as the plate gets wider, so at the *same pressure* a wide plate sinks slightly **more** than a narrow one (a narrow plate is helped by soil shearing around its edges: it behaves stiffer). That is the opposite of the intuition "wide floats", and on clay, where `kphi` dominates, it is only a 6% effect (checked in `width_helps_through_pressure_not_through_the_kc_over_b_term`). Wide tracks float because the same weight over more width is **less pressure**, and sinkage grows steeply with pressure, so that wins by a large factor (an 11 t tank sinks 0.9 mm on clay where four 0.4 m tyres sink 112 mm). Inverting it gives the number a designer wants, the sinkage for a given ground pressure `z = (p / (kc/b + kphi))^(1/n)`. *Graphics analogy:* ground pressure is weight over footprint, like spreading the same paint over a bigger brush; Bekker is the response curve of the canvas.

**As a friction wedge (Mohr-Coulomb).** Soil fails by sliding along a plane. The most shear it can carry is `tau_max = c + p tan(phi)`: a cohesion `c` (clay glues itself together) plus friction proportional to the pressure pushing the two sides together. Sand has `c` near 0 and a large `phi`; wet clay the reverse. So a heavy tank in clay gets traction mostly from cohesion times *area* (width and length matter), in sand from weight.

## 2. Shear needs displacement before it delivers strength (Janosi-Hanamoto)
Soil does not hand over `tau_max` instantly: it takes some sliding of the track shoe over it, and the stress rises as `tau = tau_max (1 - e^(-j/K))` with `j` the shear displacement and `K` a soil constant (about 2.5 cm for most soils, a few mm for a stiff surface). *Graphics analogy:* the saturating tone-mapping curve; the first part is nearly linear (the soil is a stiff spring, stress `tau_max j / K`), then it rolls off to a ceiling. For a track the shear displacement grows along the footprint (`j = i x` for a slip `i`, `x` measured from the front of the patch): the front of the track barely shears and the rear delivers the full strength, so thrust is `H = (A c + W tan phi)[1 - (K/(iL))(1 - e^(-iL/K))]`, rising with slip and saturating. The code reproduces that integral (S4) with 24 samples; a short track at low slip delivers a smaller share of the ceiling because `iL/K` is small.

## 3. Why a wide, long footprint floats, and where the energy goes
A vehicle sinks until the soil's pressure at that depth carries its weight. The depth it sinks to is also the depth of the rut it must make as it moves, and making a rut is **compaction resistance**: the work of pressing soil down, `b ∫ p dz = b (kc/b + kphi) z^(n+1)/(n+1)` per metre travelled (for `n = 1` that is `b p z / 2`: half a rectangle of pressure times depth, the area under a straight spring curve). More sinkage costs more than linearly (`z^(n+1)`), so adding weight past a threshold turns "slow" into "stuck": that is the **ladder** chart. Belly drag is the next step: when the hull sits on the soil its whole underside bears and shears, and the cohesion of the soil acts over that big plate even at almost no pressure, so a naive switch makes a jump of kilonewtons the instant the hull touches. We ease the contact in over 3 cm of extra sinkage (a smoothstep: zero force and zero slope at the onset, the largest step in the sweep is 0.27% of the final value) because a cliff in a force is a numerical hammer. In the ladder on snow (`media/ladder_sinkage.png`) the model follows the soil theory exactly until the hull touches (at 28 t, 47 kPa), and then the belly takes a growing share of the weight so sinkage grows more slowly than the theory says; the net drawbar pull peaks near 60 t and then falls (`media/ladder_pull.png`) because the belly drags more than the tracks gain.

## 4. Skid steering is scrub, and a pivot is the most expensive thing a tank does
A tracked vehicle has no steering wheel: it turns by driving the two belts at different speeds, so the shoes *slide sideways* over the ground. The slide of each sample is `u_y = omega x` (yaw rate times distance from the centre), always saturating the friction, so each sample resists with `mu p` and the total moment is `∫ mu p |x| dx`; for a uniform load that is `mu W L / 4`, **independent of how fast you turn** (Coulomb friction). The drive has to supply that moment through *unequal thrust* on the two tracks, which needs slip, which is power lost to the ground: a pivot turn cost 10x a straight run in the S3 rig, and a long footprint (L/B large) turns noticeably wider than the belt speeds predict (`R = (B/2)(vo + vi)/(vo - vi)` is only exact for a short footprint). *Graphics analogy:* the kinematic radius is the ideal pose; friction is the physics-based correction that makes the real pose lag it.

## 5. Two numerical lessons (spike S3)
A shear displacement behaves as a spring, so a tank on it can ring at about `sqrt(mu g / K)` (5 Hz), parked on a slope or driving along. Real soil has internal damping; the model adds a viscous stress sized from the spring (damping ratio 1) that acts only on the part of the slip velocity that changes faster than half a second. That is a high-pass filter: a steady slip, which is what a thrust-slip curve measures, passes untouched, and the ring is removed. (Damping the slip velocity itself is the obvious choice and is wrong: it adds a speed-proportional drag that bends the thrust curve, which we measured as +7.8% at 5% slip.) And a shear state that moves along the footprint needs a half-cell correction so that the cell-centre stress, not the exit-face stress, is used: without it a low-slip thrust curve is off by a cell of displacement, large when `K` is only two cells.

## 6. Two things the benches show
- **Thrust against slip** (`media/thrust_slip.png`): the curve rises steeply and flattens as `1 - K/(iL)(1 - e^(-iL/K))` says; the clayey soil and the snow, very different in sinkage, deliver nearly the same drawbar pull at 15 t (cohesion times area plus weight times `tan phi` happen to match), but the snow pays 3.7 kN just to roll (`R_c` at 0.3 m sinkage, `n = 1.6`).
- **Pivot** (`media/pivot_response.png`): with the belts at plus and minus the same speed, the yaw rate that the hull settles at hardly depends on the friction coefficient (asphalt and dirt differ by 3%). Both the scrub moment and the thrust that must overcome it scale with `mu`, so `mu` cancels: what is left is geometry (footprint length against gauge). A long, narrow-gauge tank is a sluggish pivoter on any firm surface.
