# Theory: the replay as a G-buffer for time

*(Written in stages; this is the first, about how a replay is stored. Later PRs add skeleton, interpolation and the wagon-wheel effect.)*

**A replay is written once and read by many passes**, the way a G-buffer is rendered once and read by lighting, fog and post-effects. The simulation is the expensive
geometry pass; the browser viewer, the Godot player and the validation harness are the cheap passes that only read. That is why the viewer is not allowed to compute physics: anything it invented would disagree with the other readers.

**Quantisation is a precision budget.** Storing every number as a 64-bit float would be wasteful: a position that is wrong by half a millimetre is invisible, so we store whole millimetres.
The budget is set by what you will *do* with the number. Millimetres for position; 0.1 milliradian (2 pi over 65536) for an aiming angle, because a gun-laying error plot has to resolve errors that small.
Rotation uses **smallest-three**: a unit quaternion has four numbers but they must satisfy w^2+x^2+y^2+z^2 = 1, so the largest can be recomputed from the other three; we store which one it was and the three small ones (all within +-0.707).
Resolution: 16 bits over a range of 1.41 is a step of 2.2e-5, which is 0.003 degrees; 10 bits would be a step of 1.4e-3, about 0.14 degrees, visibly wobbly on a long gun barrel. (The test `rotation_round_trips_within_0p01_degrees` guards this.)

**Delta coding is prediction.** Consecutive frames are alike, so store only the *surprise*. If the wheel turned 0.5 rad last frame, it probably turns about 0.5 again: predict
"same step as last time" and store the error, which is near zero. That is the same idea as video P-frames (and PNG's filters). Where motion is smooth we predict by extrapolating the last two frames; where values are noisy (tyre forces) the
extrapolation would amplify noise, so there we predict "same as last frame". Small numbers need fewer bytes in a varint (7 bits per byte), and runs of unchanged values collapse to two bytes. A keyframe every second restarts the prediction from zero so a viewer can jump anywhere.

## A vehicle is a skeleton
The hull, the turret, the gun and every wheel form a **joint tree**, exactly like a character rig. Each node stores where it sits when its joint is at zero (the rest pose); each frame supplies one number per joint:
an angle for a spinning or steering node, a distance for a sliding one (suspension travel, recoil). Forward kinematics walks the tree from the hull outwards: *travel first, then steer, then spin*.
A wheel's world position is the hull pose, moved down the suspension arm by the travel, turned by the steer angle, and only then spun. Swap the order and the wheel would spin about a steering axis that has not turned yet.
The viewer does nothing else; it never decides where a wheel is, it just evaluates the chain the simulation recorded. `smoke.mjs` proves each joint is wired by moving it alone and watching the pixels (or, for a sliding joint, the exact distance).

## Interpolation: why a viewer can run at any frame rate
The file stores 30 snapshots per second but the screen may refresh at 60 or 144 Hz, and a video may be rendered at 24. The viewer blends the two nearest snapshots: positions and joints linearly, rotations by **slerp** (spherical
interpolation: it moves along the shortest arc of the rotation sphere at constant angular speed, where a plain lerp of quaternion numbers would speed up and slow down). Because wheel spin is stored *unwrapped* (never reset at 2 pi), linear blending is correct; a wrapped angle would sweep the wheel backwards through a whole turn between two frames.

## The wagon-wheel effect is aliasing, not a bug
A wheel turning 11 rad/s advances 0.37 rad (21 degrees) per 30 Hz frame. If a wheel has four identical spokes (symmetry every 90 degrees) and it turns 85 degrees between frames, the eye reads it as 5 degrees *backwards*: with identical spokes, "advanced 85" and "went back 5" look the same. This is the sampling theorem at work, the same reason a texture shimmers when
sampled too coarsely: a signal can only be recovered if you sample at more than twice its highest frequency. The data in the file is right (the spin angle is stored unwrapped); the picture is what any 30 Hz sampling of a symmetric rotating object gives. That is why the box wheels carry one asymmetric lug: it makes direction readable, and the viewer can render extra in-between frames from the interpolated spin if you want a smooth wheel.

## Live: drawing a stream you do not control
The test-drive page is a viewer of a replay that is still being written: the server sends a snapshot about every 1/30 s and the screen refreshes at 60 Hz or more. The page keeps the last two snapshots and draws a point **between them**, one snapshot behind the newest, the same interpolation as for a file. The price is 33 ms of delay; the gain is motion without stutter, and it never has to guess the future (extrapolating would overshoot at every bump and every reset).
The camera is a **critically damped follower**: each frame it closes a fraction `1 - exp(-dt / tau)` of the gap to where it wants to be. The `exp` makes the smoothing independent of the frame rate (a fixed fraction per frame would feel different at 30 and 144 Hz); `tau` is how long it takes to close about two thirds of the gap. The heading uses a long `tau` (0.45 s) so a bend does not whip the picture round, the height a medium one (0.35 s) so bumps do not shake the horizon, and a jump of more than 12 m (a reset) is a cut, not a glide.
The pedals travel the other way on their own 30 Hz timer, not in the draw loop: a slow computer drawing ten pictures a second must still keep feeding the server, whose 0.5 s pedal timeout is its safety net (like a dead-man's handle).

## Reading a tornado and a ladder
**A tornado is a sensitivity test.** Raise one lever by 10%, hold everything else, run the benchmark twice and diff the results, the way you would nudge one shader parameter and diff two renders. The bar is the change as a percentage of the baseline, which makes it a **power-law exponent in disguise**: if a benchmark follows `B ~ L^e` then `+10%` on the lever gives about `10 e` percent. A constant-power launch has `t = m v^2 / (2 P)`, so engine power should show `e = -1`: the 0-48 km/h panel should read near `-9%` for power, and it does (`-9.2%` on the Mule), while vehicle mass reads `+7.8%`, under the `+10%` the table expects (`t ~ m`). A bar much longer or shorter than `10 e` is a finding: another effect is at work, and the force ledger says which limit decided the run.
**The same lever can be right for one vehicle and wrong for another.** The table's sign for a lever is only true in one *regime*, the set of conditions that decides which limit applies: a vehicle that slides before it tips should not care how high its centre of mass is (side-slope panel: the Hauler, which tips, moves `-7.5%` as the table says; the Mule and the Scout, which slide first, should not move and move `-0.6%` and `-0.7%`, just over the 0.5% dead band, so they carry a red cross), and a skidpad run limited by engine power does not care about the chassis. That is why the runner decides the verdict, not the chart, and why a red cross on one vehicle's bar beside a clean bar for another vehicle is the normal shape of a real result.
**Why the failures are drawn.** A *dead lever* (moves nothing on a vehicle) and an *orphan benchmark* (nothing moves it) are the two ways a model can lie by omission: a slider that does nothing teaches the player a falsehood, and an outcome with no cause cannot be designed. So a failed check is drawn even when its bar is tiny, below-threshold levers are counted instead of dropped, a measured zero is a 2 px stub, and the benchmarks that have no test yet are named in the footer, because a missing measurement must not look like a clean one.
**A ladder is a one-parameter animation, one rung per frame.** Add a tonne at a time and watch sinkage. Bekker's soil law `z = (p / (kc/b + kphi))^(1/n)` has `n < 1` for soft ground, and `p` is weight over footprint, so `z` grows like `mass^(1/n)`: faster than the load, the curve bends upward, and a vehicle passes from "fine" to "bogged" over a very short step. The dashed line is what the theory predicts for the same ground and footprint, the shaded band is how far the simulation may stray before we stop trusting it (the acceptance test), and the red line is where a wheel can no longer climb out of its own rut.
**Colour has one job per channel.** In both charts colour is the vehicle and nothing else: the sign of an effect is position (left or right of zero), the difference between measurement and theory is line style. The palette is checked by computation, not by eye: the validator simulates colour-blind vision and measures the distance between each pair of colours in OKLab, a space built so that equal distances look about equally different (the same reason a graphics pipeline blends in a perceptual space). Adjacent colours are validated for eight series, which is enough for lines; a ninth vehicle becomes a new chart, not a new hue.

## A design is a function of its levers
The Workshop is `design(levers) = (rig, skin, numbers)`: take the base vehicle, apply each lever as a **factor** (1.0 means "as authored", 1.1 means ten percent more), compile, bake the body, run the proving ground. Factors rather than absolute values because they compose (apply wheelbase then mass, in either order, to the same base), they do not depend on which vehicle is the base, and the value 1 is always a safe reset. A change the physics cannot honour is refused with its reason instead of being clamped: a clamped slider silently shows a design that is not the one you built. The body has two costs worth separating, the shape (milliseconds) and the baked ambient-occlusion look (seconds, proportional to the number of rays); a preview bake with 16 rays costs a sixteenth of the full one and looks the same until you zoom, which is the same trade as a proxy against a final render.
Because the design is a pure function of the base, the levers and the bake quality (same inputs, same bytes: nothing in it reads a clock or a random number), the server may remember every answer it has given and hand the remembered one back, which is memoisation: dragging a slider across values it has already seen costs nothing. The rule that makes this safe is the same one that makes a replay safe, determinism, and it is why the project forbids hidden state in the simulation.
An interactive page that asks a slow server a question every time a slider moves has one classic failure: answers arriving out of order, so the picture shows an old design while the slider shows a new one. The cure is a sequence number: each request is stamped, the page remembers the newest stamp, and an answer for an older stamp is thrown away on arrival. It is the same idea as cancelling a viewport render when the camera moves again, and the same reason a slider is debounced: do not ask a question you will not wait for the answer to.

## A scoreboard row is an experiment with a control
Every row on the Workshop's board is a two-render comparison: the same test on the same proving ground, once on the vehicle as authored (the **control**) and once on the design, with one thing different. The control is measured once and kept, the way a reference image is rendered once and every change is diffed against it; that is safe only because the simulation is deterministic, so the control cannot drift between runs.
The change is shown as a **percentage of the base** so that a time in seconds, a distance in metres and an angle in degrees can sit in one column and be read the same way. The sign of a percentage does not say whether the result is good: a shorter 0-48 km/h time is better and a steeper maximum gradient is better, so each benchmark carries its own direction and the row says "better" or "worse" in words, with a tick or a cross as a second cue (a colour alone is lost on a colour-blind reader). Under half a percent the runner's own dead band says "no change": a difference smaller than the resolution of the measurement is not a finding.
When a slider moves, the numbers on the board are **greyed rather than cleared**: they still answer the previous question, and showing them in full ink next to the new body would answer a question nobody asked. The same sequence number that drops a stale picture drops a stale board, so the body and the numbers on screen always describe one design.
A board can surprise you, and that is its job. On the Hauler the 0-48 km/h time falls smoothly with engine power (-6%, -11%, -15%, -18% and -25% at x1.1, x1.2, x1.3, x1.4 and x2.0: more power, shorter launch, as `t = m v^2 / 2P` says), but the maximum gradient jumps about (+14%, 0, +6%, -2%, 0 on the same steps) and the braking distance, which power should hardly touch, is 4.0% longer at x1.5. A gradient is found by *bisection*: try a slope, see whether the vehicle climbs it, halve the interval. That works only if pass/fail is monotone in slope (everything below some slope passes, everything above fails); if a launch that grips on a steep slope can spin on a gentler one, the search lands in different places for nearly identical vehicles, and a small difference on the board is the benchmark's noise, not the vehicle's physics. I do not know which it is here. The board only reports it, the force ledgers of the runs would say, and it is written up for VALIDATION as a question, not hidden and not fixed in the viewer.


## A track is a motion path
A tank track is a chain of rigid links wrapped round a set of wheels. Drawing it is not a physics problem (the links are not simulated one by one); it is **instancing along a curve by arc length**, the same thing an animation package calls a motion path. Two decisions make it work.
**The curve.** A taut belt is a rubber band stretched round nails: it touches only the wheels on the outside of the shape they make (the *convex hull* of the wheel circles) and runs in straight lines between them, bending round each wheel it touches. Between two neighbouring circles the straight part is their outer common tangent; round a circle the belt follows an arc. Both have closed forms, so the length is exact: for two equal wheels of radius `r` a distance `d` apart it is `2 d + 2 pi r`, two straight runs and one full circle's worth of arc. A wheel that is lifted *inside* that shape (a road wheel on a bump) is simply not touched and drops out of the curve: the belt does not fail, it ignores the nail.
**The parameter.** Put `n` links on the curve a distance `L / n` apart (arc length, not angle: a link moves the same distance along a straight run as round a wheel), and make the whole pattern slide by exactly the distance the driving sprocket rolls, its radius times its spin. Then the belt moves as the simulated sprocket turns, with no extra data in the replay: the sprocket's spin is already one of the joints. On a recorded drive the sprocket's rolling distance and the hull's travel agree to a few percent at every speed, which is the physics saying the track does not skid: the links on the ground stand still on the ground. A belt is also *periodic*, so after a distance `L` every link is home, which is what the tests check exactly.
**Winding.** A loop can be walked clockwise or counter-clockwise. The two carriers in the repository walk theirs in opposite senses, and the maths written for one refuses the other. The cure is a reflection: mirror the picture (negate one axis), where the loop winds the right way, build the band, and mirror the result back; distances, arc lengths and tangents survive a reflection, so nothing else changes. One care: a reflection also turns a mesh inside out (its triangles' winding flips), so a link is placed with a rotation chosen to point the same way, never with the mirror itself.

## Why a camera shutter does not cure the wagon wheel, and what does
A video is a set of samples of the world, one every `1 / f` seconds. A pattern that repeats every `P` metres and moves at speed `v` flickers at each pixel at the frequency `v / P`, and a sampler running at `f` can only tell frequencies below `f / 2` apart (the Nyquist limit): above it the samples are exactly the samples of a slower, or backwards, pattern, and that is the wagon wheel. It is the same thing as moire when a fine checkerboard is drawn at too low a resolution, only in time instead of in space, and it has the same cure in principle: filter before you sample (a texture's mipmap does this in space), because after sampling the information is already gone.
Motion blur is a filter in time: average the picture over the time the shutter is open, `T`. That is a box filter, and a box is a poor low-pass: its response is `sinc(frequency x T)`, so at the frequency where aliasing starts (`f / 2`) a 180 degree shutter (`T = 1 / 2f`, the film default) keeps `sinc(1/4)`, 90% of the pattern, and even a shutter open for the whole frame keeps `sinc(1/2)`, 64%. That is why film still shows wagon wheels and why a blur stops being the answer here.
The cure used for the tank track is to filter the *signal* itself with a cutoff matched to the frame rate. The links alternate light and dark; the contrast of that pattern is made a raised cosine of how far the belt moves between two displayed frames: full at rest, half at half a pitch a frame, zero from one pitch a frame (the pattern would then start to move the wrong way). Smooth, so nothing pops, and slowing the playback brings the pattern back because the belt then moves less per frame. The price is honest and familiar: above about 16 km/h at 30 frames a second (32 km/h at 60) the track is a plain dark band, like a real one in a video of a tank at speed, and the wheels and the hull still show the speed.

## Reading the footprint: pressure, sinkage and why a track spreads the weight
A wheel or a track carries the vehicle's weight through a patch of ground, and what the ground does depends on **pressure**, force over area, not on the force alone. A tracked carrier of 10.3 tonnes weighs 101 kN and rests on two belts of 2.9 m by 0.38 m, 2.2 m2 in all, so its average ground pressure is about 46 kPa; in the recording on the firm road the 24 cells average 4.1 kN, 44 kPa on their footprints (their sum is the vehicle's weight, 98 kN of 101), and the highest is 59 kPa. Narrow the belt and the same weight sits on less area, so the pressure rises in proportion: this is why the width of a track is a design lever.
Soft ground answers pressure with **sinkage**. Bekker's law for a plate, `z = (p / (kc / b + k_phi))^(1/n)`, says the depth grows with pressure and that a narrower plate (smaller `b`, larger `kc / b`) sinks less per unit pressure but is loaded harder; the simulator evaluates it cell by cell along the belt, and the ribbon beside the belt is those depths drawn where they happen, the scale getting darker as the cell goes deeper (a *sequential* scale: only magnitude, no sign). The picture of the mud crossing shows the pattern a person would expect and a number alone would hide: the cells sink from the front backwards as the belt enters the pit, because the front is the first to meet the soft ground.
**Peak is not average.** At the far bank of the pit, at 48 km/h, the front 25 cm cell of the right track carries 354 kN, three and a half times the whole vehicle's weight, which is 3,765 kPa on its footprint, while the eleven cells behind it, in the mud, carry 6 to 16 kN each: a collision with the bank, not weight. The HUD prints the highest cell, not the mean, because that is the number that breaks things, and the bar is capped on screen so one cell does not fill the picture. A cell that is not touching the ground is not drawn at all: at 48 km/h over rough ground a tracked vehicle is often partly airborne, and the overlay shows it.
